# First-person sleeves, cuffs and gloves, October 15, 2026

The owner's note: "sleeves / shirts look like tubes, especially when wielding a weapon." In first person the weapon view (`crysis/viewmodel.js`) drew each sleeve as a round cylinder of kit cloth, always the same olive, cut short of the wrist with a round cuff, and the fists (`combat/melee-view.js`) drew a primitive cylinder that no longer lined up with the anatomical forearms from #36 (it floated in front of the view during a straight punch, and the real forearms were bare under any outfit).

Both now dress your arms from a shared garment module, `island/src/people/sleeve.js`, cut from what you actually wear.

## What you see

- **Your own clothes.** The sleeves come from the avatar's outfit (`P.outfit`, the DNA style from `people/wardrobe.js`, or a fighter's kit from `combat/outfits.js`): the top's sleeve, and a jacket's over it. Colours, two-colour blocking, prints and patterns match what the body wears in third person.
- **Shape.** The cross-section is measured on this body's own forearm at rest and bridged as cloth bridges it (a convex hull of the section), so it is flatter on the inside and fuller over the muscle on the outside, and tapers from the elbow to the wrist. The cloth stands off by the garment's ease (a tee 6 mm, a shirt 9 mm, a hoodie 12 mm and more when oversized, a shell 16 mm), with more room at the back of the arm than the front.
- **Hems and cuffs, with thickness.** Every edge rolls round the cloth's own thickness and turns back up inside, so no edge reads as paper:
  - ribbed knit cuff (hoodie, sweatshirt, knit, track top, bomber, puffer), with the sleeve gathered and bloused over it;
  - buttoned band with a placket and its small button (shirts, flannel, denim jacket, chore and utility jackets, police shirts);
  - tabbed band with a hook-and-loop strip (camouflage combat shirts and jackets);
  - bound storm cuff with a tab (shells);
  - coverstitched hem (long-sleeved tees);
  - plain band (blazer, leather);
  - rolled sleeves: a thick band of three folds over the elbow, curled under at its lower edge, each fold showing the cloth's paler reverse, with the bare forearm below. Outfits mark them with `sleeves: 'elbow', roll: true`, so third person shows sleeves to the elbow and keeps the forearm's skin.
- **Layering.** A jacket over a long-sleeved top shows both hems at the wrist: the top's cuff about 13 mm below the jacket's, the jacket cut over the top's sleeve (never through it).
- **Gloves** when the outfit has them: tactical gloves for soldiers, militia and the police tactical team (stretch back, synthetic suede palm, the seam where they meet, moulded rubber knuckles, a neoprene cuff with a strap). The glove is a shell over the hand's own refined skin, and the skin under it is left out, so it costs about what it replaces.
- **Skin where it shows.** A tee or a rolled sleeve leaves the forearm as the anatomical hand's own skin, with its tattoos; nothing is drawn over it. Long sleeves end at the wrist bone, and the skin hidden under a long sleeve (above the cuff and its ride) is left out of the hands' mesh, so nothing can poke through and nothing is drawn twice.

## Folds that follow the arm

No cloth simulation. Each frame `pose()` reads a few numbers per arm off the bones, eased so nothing pops:

| | read from | drives |
|---|---|---|
| bend | the angle at the elbow between the shoulder (where it sits in the view) and the wrist | the fan of folds at the inside of the elbow, and deeper stacking at the cuff |
| twist | the palm's turn round the forearm against the elbow's hinge, less the same at rest | spiral folds along the forearm (the support hand under a handguard) |
| reach | how far the forearm points forward | drag lines down the back of the forearm, and the sleeve riding up |
| aim | the view's own aim (0 to 1) | the cuff riding up the wrist (up to 7 mm for a rib, 12 mm for a tee, 18 mm for a shirt or jacket) |
| down | the world's down, in the forearm's frame at rest | fullness hanging on the down side, and stacking at the cuff when the hand hangs |

The vertex shader displaces the cloth along its normal from these and a little remembered creasing, never inward by more than half the ease, and tilts the normals by the same field (three samples per vertex). Creases are shaded darker in the fragment shader.

## The cloth

Procedural detail at real scale, faded out by screen footprint before it can shimmer: cotton jersey loops, a sweatshirt's heathered face, shirting, ripstop twill, nylon ripstop with a sheen, denim's twill with slubs and pale worn crests, canvas, fleece and brushed flannel with fuzz (sheen), leather grain, rib knit, suiting. Prints: camouflage blotches at their printed size (about 5 to 12 cm), plaid and check on the grain, stripes, pinstripes, a track stripe down the outside, dots, a puffer's baffles. Seams run down the underside of the sleeve with their stitching (twin topstitching in gold on denim). Worn clothes get a grimy cuff edge, rubbed crests, a few marks and pilling; police uniforms stay pressed. The material is `MeshPhysicalMaterial` with the shader added, lit by the same lights as the hands' skin.

The long-sleeved outfits come out 1.4k to 3.4k triangles over #36's 27.8k figure (the garment is about 1.2k to 1.7k a sleeve, less the skin it hides); a tee is under it. Desktop sleeves use 16 sides round the arm; phones and the governor's low and minimal tiers (`qualityNow()`) use 10, fewer rings and skip the cloth's fine bump. The quality listener swaps the shading when the tier changes; the cut stays as built. The per-frame path allocates nothing.

If it remains, the fragment fold slope is the other candidate (phones do not show it).
- Captures: BEFORE is the full desktop set (75 images, phone BEFORE from the first run was overwritten); AFTER is a 19-image matched subset (desktop and phone) from the last iteration, not the full matrix. The harness (`sleeve-browser.mjs`) produces the full matrix when the machine allows.
- The bare forearm's own skin shading (warm, smooth) is the skin shader's, unchanged here.

- The upper arm is not drawn in first person, so a short sleeve's hem (above the elbow) is not either; in every pose captured the elbow is out of view.
- The player's outfit is read when the arms are built; a mid-session `redress` of the player (none exists today) would need the arms rebuilt.
- Cloth against the weapon is not compressed by contact; the ease is small enough not to show clipping in the captured poses.
